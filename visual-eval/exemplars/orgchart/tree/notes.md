# Org chart — exemplar target

**Scenario.** Lumen Health, a seventeen-person health-software company, drawn the way an HR lead would paste it into a handbook: chief executive on top, four executives under her, their directors below, team leads at the fourth level. It exercises the four things that separate a real org chart from a generic tree — a chief of staff attached to the side of the CEO rather than hanging under her as a report, an unfilled vacancy, a line that is not a reporting line (the head of design has design-system authority over the web lead, who works for someone else), and department colour coding with a key.

**Palette.** Ink `#12161C` names, slate `#5C6672` job titles, muted `#8B95A2` captions, hairline `#DDE2E9` card borders, connector `#98A3B1` reporting lines, page `#F7F8FA` behind white cards.

Departments: Executive `#39435A`, Product `#613E98`, Engineering `#2D6EA3`, Operations `#31865E`, Revenue `#B4762F`. Two rules shaped that set. First, the four *functions* sit far apart on the colour wheel — violet, blue, green, amber, never less than 55 degrees between neighbours — so no two of them blur together when the chart is shrunk to a slide thumbnail. Executive is deliberately not a fifth hue: it is a near-neutral graphite-navy, because the top of the chart is not a function alongside the other four, and keeping it grey leaves the four working hues room to spread out. Second, the five are stepped in brightness (graphite darkest, then violet, blue, green, amber lightest, at least five points apart on the perceptual lightness scale), so they stay in a readable order when the page is printed or photocopied in black and white.

One accent lives outside that set — amber `#B08A2E` on cream `#FDF9EF` — used for exactly one thing, the open position. It is the only filled card on the page, so the vacancy is the first thing an HR reader's eye lands on.

**Type scale.** Title 22/600, caption 12/400, name 14/600 (13 on the smaller cards), job title 11.5/400, department 9.5/600 uppercase with 0.7 tracking. The name is always the heaviest thing in a card; the department the lightest.

**Cards.** One corner radius (10), one border weight (1), no shadows. Each card is white with a 3px department-coloured bar across its top edge, tucked inside the corner radius, and an initials disc filled in the same colour with white letters. Department is written under the name in plain grey uppercase — the word carries the meaning, the colour does not need to repeat it. That is one colour encoding per card (bar and disc share a single hue) rather than three, which keeps the card quiet enough that the person's name stays the loudest thing on it. Executives get a 232x78 card, everyone below 200x66; that size step signals the level before you read a single connector. The vacancy swaps the initials for a dashed circle with a plus, dashes its border and fills it cream.

**Layout.** Buchheim–Walker tidy tree, top-down: each manager centred over its children, dropping to a shared horizontal bus that then drops into each child's top edge, all right angles. The two four-person engineering groups would have pushed the drawing past 2,000px wide, so those leaves use the stacked variant the standard permits for wide leaf groups — a short vertical spine from the parent's lower-left with horizontal stubs into each card. The chief of staff hangs off the CEO's right edge on one horizontal line at the same height, the Microsoft/Lucidchart assistant convention, which is what tells it apart from a direct report. The dashed design-system line leaves the head of design downward, runs left through the clear band between levels three and four, turns down the corridor beside the engineering stack and enters the web lead's right edge with an arrowhead — thinner than the reporting lines, tinted with the Product hue so you can see where it starts, crossing nothing.

**Collisions.** Positions were solved from measured text widths by a script that then tested every text box against every line, every other text box and the canvas edge. Zero collisions.

**Departure from the standard.** The standard offers role icons (crown for the CEO, gear for the CTO) as an alternative to initials; this drawing uses initials throughout. Fifteen small pictograms on top of five department colours makes the page busier without telling the reader anything the job title has not already said.
