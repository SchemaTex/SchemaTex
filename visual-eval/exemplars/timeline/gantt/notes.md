The planned schedule for moving a clinic onto an electronic health record system, January to May 2026: eleven tasks and three milestones grouped into four phases (Planning, Build, Training, Go-live). It follows the Gantt chart convention popularised by Wallace Clark's *The Gantt Chart: A Working Tool of Management* (1922; https://archive.org/details/ganttchartworkin00claruoft) and still used in project-management software: one row per task, task names in a table on the left, time across the top, a bar from each task's start to its end, and a diamond for each milestone (https://www.netronic.com/hubfs/2017%20Relaunch/Resources/Documents/For%20Developers/Ebook%20-%20The%20Ultimate%20Gantt%20Chart%20Cheat%20Sheet.pdf). The dates are typed in, not computed; a computed critical path belongs to the PERT diagram.

Why it works as the exemplar for this type:

- Every task gets its own row. The left table repeats the task name and its start – end dates, so the schedule can be read without measuring bars.
- Phases are bold header rows on a faint band, carrying a black summary bar with downward end caps that spans the phase's first start to its last finish. This is the standard summary-task mark.
- The time header has three tiers: year, month names, and the Monday date of each week. Week gridlines run through the whole chart, so any bar end can be traced to its week.
- Bars are thin (16 px) tinted rounded rectangles in the phase's hue. The only text beside a bar is its duration in working days in small muted type, never the task name a second time.
- Milestones are solid black diamonds centred on their day, with the date in bold to the right. They sit on their own rows, so they never overlap a bar.
- The legend at the bottom explains the task bar, the phase summary bar and the milestone diamond.
